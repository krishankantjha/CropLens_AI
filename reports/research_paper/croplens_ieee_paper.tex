\begin{abstract}
Agricultural spot prices move constantly --- across commodities, markets, seasons, weather, arrivals, and demand cycles. A single point forecast can't tell a farmer or buyer what the downside risk actually looks like. This paper presents CropLens AI --- a leakage-controlled forecasting and market-intelligence system built for India's APMC markets. It combines historical prices and arrivals with weather, satellite vegetation, calendar, and spatial signals, all organized under a 47-feature contract. Targets are built one day ahead within each commodity--mandi series, chronological order is preserved, and the 2025 period is kept fully untouched as a holdout. The core forecasting model trains independent LightGBM regressors for the 10th, 50th, and 90th quantiles, fixes crossing violations through monotonic rearrangement, and calibrates the resulting interval using validation-only Mondrian CQR. The canonical benchmark compares Ridge, Naive Persistence, XGBoost, CatBoost, ARIMA, LSTM, GRU, and Temporal Fusion Transformer models under a common evaluation scope. On the frozen 2025 holdout, Ridge provides the lowest aggregate point error, whereas the LightGBM system supplies calibrated uncertainty with 79.85\% empirical coverage against an 80\% target and zero post-rearrangement crossings. The results make the case for reporting both accuracy and uncertainty together --- and subgroup, spatial, and residual checks surface weaknesses that a single pooled score would otherwise hide.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Agricultural prices are shaped by a mix of supply, demand, weather, transport, storage, seasonality, and available information. Indian Agricultural Produce Market Committee markets are important physical venues where farmers and traders exchange commodities and observe daily wholesale prices. That creates a real timing problem --- decisions often have to be made before the next day's price is even known.

For many of these decisions, a single point estimate just isn't enough. A farmer deciding whether to harvest and transport a perishable crop needs to understand the potential downside price, while a procurement agency needs a realistic price range to plan budgets and storage. The same central forecast can support very different decisions depending on the width and reliability of its uncertainty interval.

CropLens AI tackles this as a full software and machine-learning system, tying together data ingestion, feature construction, model training, uncertainty calibration, inference, caching, and the frontend. The system is designed around a strict separation between historical training data and live serving data. That separation matters: a model can look accurate offline and still fail in production if feature names, ordering, missing-value handling, or target definitions drift.

The core contribution is bringing multimodal agricultural features, multi-quantile forecasting, conformal calibration, and operational validation together into one evidence-based system. The work does not claim a new learning algorithm or state-of-the-art performance. Instead, it presents and evaluates a reproducible domain-specific pipeline in which target construction, feature contracts, chronological evaluation, and artifact validation are treated as first-class research requirements.

There are four main contributions. First, the system organizes market, weather, satellite, calendar, and spatial information into a canonical feature representation covering 10 commodities and 10 mandis. Second, it trains separate LightGBM models for the 10th, 50th, and 90th conditional quantiles and records the tuning evidence for each one. Third, it combines rearrangement and conformal calibration to produce ordered, empirically assessable intervals. And finally, it benchmarks the production model against linear, tree-based, statistical, recurrent, and attention-based approaches, while surfacing the limitations of pooled metrics and heterogeneous deployment conditions.

\section{Related Work}
Autoregressive time-series models have been the backbone of agricultural forecasting for decades. ARIMA and related Box--Jenkins methods are interpretable and useful for series with sufficient historical structure \cite{box2015time}. Their univariate form, however, makes it difficult to incorporate a large set of weather, satellite, arrival, calendar, and spatial variables.

A different line of work turned to tree-based models, which fit flexible nonlinear patterns directly to structured data. XGBoost relies on regularized gradient boosting \cite{chen2016xgboost}, LightGBM on efficient histogram-based learning \cite{ke2017lightgbm}, and CatBoost on ordered boosting with built-in categorical handling \cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost}. These approaches are well suited to tabular agricultural data, but their validity depends on how features and temporal splits are constructed.

More recently, a third family of models has approached the problem sequentially. LSTM networks use gated memory to model temporal dependence \cite{hochreiter1997long}, while GRU networks provide a more compact gated architecture \cite{cho2014learning}. Temporal Fusion Transformers combine recurrent processing, attention, and variable-selection mechanisms for forecasting \cite{lim2021temporal}. We include these here strictly as controlled benchmarks --- not because they were tuned as extensively as the production LightGBM models.

Quantile regression works by minimizing asymmetric loss to estimate a conditional quantile \cite{koenker1978regression}. Independently estimated quantiles can cross, producing invalid intervals. Rearrangement provides a monotonic correction \cite{chernozhukov2010quantile}. Conformalized Quantile Regression uses held-out calibration information to improve empirical coverage \cite{romano2019conformalized}; broader conformal-prediction literature describes the distribution-free motivation and limitations of such procedures \cite{angelopoulos2021gentle}. We apply these ideas directly to a multi-market agricultural forecasting workflow.

\section{System and Data Architecture}
\subsection{End-to-End System}
The repository runs on a FastAPI backend and a React frontend. The backend alone covers API routing, services, database models, migrations, model loading, live sync, caching, alerts, and reporting. SQLAlchemy provides database access and Alembic manages schema migrations. Redis is configured for caching and rate-limiting-related operations, while the application includes an in-memory fallback for selected development conditions.

The application loads a versioned production model bundle through a model registry. The registry requires the three LightGBM quantile artifacts and metadata describing the active feature contract. Startup also validates the serving dataset. If the bundle or dataset is incomplete, the system flags a degraded state instead of falsely signaling readiness. The health endpoint reports model readiness, dataset readiness, loaded model names, feature count, and active model version.

The frontend consumes backend responses for forecasting, alerts, procurement, analytics, and reporting. Data moves through the system in a fixed order --- observations enter the ingestion layer, canonical feature logic builds the serving features, the registry loads validated artifacts, the API generates predictions, and the frontend renders the result. This keeps the interface from quietly showing stale placeholder data.

\subsection{Data Scope}
The processed dataset spans 2019--2025 across 10 commodities and 10 representative mandis. The preflight validation found 135,471 processed rows and all 47 canonical feature columns. The training pipeline reported 135,404 usable rows after strict next-day target construction, since rows missing a valid next-day target can't be used for supervised training.

\subsection{Feature and Target Contract}
The feature contract draws on four broad categories of signal --- market behavior, environmental conditions, demand cycles, and structural/spatial context --- which are broken down into the full 47-variable taxonomy below.

For commodity--market group $g$, let $p_{g,t}$ be the observed modal price at date $t$. The corrected supervised target is
\begin{equation}
y_{g,t}=p_{g,t+1},
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $t+1$ denotes the next calendar day required by the target-construction logic. The feature vector $\mathbf{x}_{g,t}$ contains only information available at the forecast cut-off. The model input order is governed by the canonical 47-feature tuple, not by automatic discovery of numeric columns. The online feature extractor combines the current observation with a history window and recomputes the same batch feature logic for the latest row. Because the contract includes a 52-week price lag, the serving history must be long enough to support the largest declared temporal dependency; a short history cannot be treated as equivalent to a complete feature vector.

The 47 variables are grouped into twelve autoregressive price-history features, four market-microstructure and rank features, five supply-and-arrival features, twelve meteorological and satellite-NDVI features, two harvest indicators, three festival-demand features, three spatial features, and six calendar or regime features. This grouping is meant for interpretation and ablation only --- it doesn't imply the groups are statistically independent.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{LightGBM Quantile Forecasting}
For quantile level $\alpha$, the pinball loss is
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\alpha}(u)=u\left(\alpha-\mathbb{I}(u<0)\right),
\label{eq:pinball}
\end{equation}
where $u=y-\hat{q}_{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})$. CropLens AI trains separate LightGBM estimators for $\alpha\in\{0.10,0.50,0.90\}$. Each quantile gets its own independent Optuna search, tuning learning rate, tree complexity, minimum child samples, subsampling, column subsampling, and regularization.

The validation partition is used for parameter selection and early stopping, while the test partition remains untouched. The implementation records the sampler seed, trial count, objective, best validation value, quantile level, and selected estimator parameters. We fix the seed at 42 and run single-threaded to keep results stable and memory use predictable. The training pipeline also writes stage checkpoints and validates checkpoint signatures, so a failure downstream doesn't force retraining of an already-completed model family. Model files and metadata are persisted immediately after the relevant family is complete, while optional diagnostics are generated later.

\subsection{Monotonic Rearrangement}
Because the three quantile models train independently, their outputs can end up logically inconsistent. For each input, the raw values are rearranged so that
\begin{equation}
\hat{q}^{*}_{0.10}(\mathbf{x})\leq\hat{q}^{*}_{0.50}(\mathbf{x})\leq\hat{q}^{*}_{0.90}(\mathbf{x}).
\label{eq:order}
\end{equation}
This fixes crossing violations before the interval ever reaches a user.

\subsection{Conformal Calibration}
Let $\hat{q}^{*}_{\ell}$ and $\hat{q}^{*}_{u}$ be the rearranged lower and upper predictions on validation data. A nonconformity score can be written as
\begin{equation}
r_i=\max\{\hat{q}^{*}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}_i)-y_i, y_i-\hat{q}^{*}_{u}(\mathbf{x}_i)\}.
\label{eq:conformal}
\end{equation}
A calibration quantile of the validation scores expands the lower and upper limits. The implementation applies the calibration after model fitting and uses metadata to carry the resulting evidence into inference. Coverage and interval width must be measured on the held-out test period; calibration results from an outdated artifact can't be reused as final evidence.

\subsection{Benchmark Models}
The repository includes Ridge, XGBoost, CatBoost, ARIMA, LSTM, GRU, and TFT components. Ridge is the regularized linear baseline. XGBoost and CatBoost cover the nonlinear tabular benchmarks, and ARIMA serves as the series-level statistical baseline. LSTM, GRU, and TFT use grouped sequence windows and the corrected next-day target. Isolation Forest is used for anomaly analysis, not price prediction, while Naive Persistence is a simple reference baseline rather than a trained model.

\section{Experimental Design}
The corrected pipeline uses a chronological split: 2019--2023 for training, 2024 for validation, and 2025 for final testing. Hyperparameter selection uses the validation period, and the test period is reserved for final evaluation. We use this over a random split because randomization risks leaking future market regimes into training.

The main point metrics are MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, with pinball loss added for the quantile models. Interval evaluation reports empirical coverage, nominal coverage, and mean prediction interval width. Crossing counts are reported before and after rearrangement. Diebold--Mariano tests compare forecast losses, while Ljung--Box tests examine residual autocorrelation \cite{diebold1995comparing,ljung1978measure}. Newey--West covariance is used where required for time-dependent loss comparisons \cite{newey1987simple}.

The evaluation also includes Leave-One-Mandi-Out validation, per-commodity and per-mandi error summaries, bootstrap confidence intervals, ablation analysis, feature attribution, and supply-shock analysis. The goal is to stop a pooled score from masking weak performance in a smaller or more volatile subgroup.

For an observed target $y_i$ and prediction $\hat{y}_i$, the primary error summaries are
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{MAE}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y_i-\hat{y}_i|,
\qquad
\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-\hat{y}_i)^2}.
\label{eq:metrics}
\end{equation}
MAPE and sMAPE are reported as percentages; MASE normalizes error against a persistence baseline. Because RMSE penalizes large deviations more than MAE does, the two can actually rank models differently in volatile markets --- which is exactly why we report several loss metrics instead of picking one.

The bootstrap analysis uses 1,000 circular temporal resamples with seven-day blocks and seed 42. Block resampling holds onto short-range temporal dependence far better than shuffling individual daily errors would. The resulting intervals quantify sampling variability within the frozen evaluation scope; they do not guarantee performance under future distribution shift.

All models share the same evaluation contract, but they aren't run as interchangeable implementations. Tabular estimators take the canonical row-wise feature vector, sequence models take grouped seven-day windows, and ARIMA works on individual commodity--mandi series. That difference matters when interpreting missing observations and coverage --- a sequence model can lose an initial window simply because it lacks enough history, while a series model may be excluded entirely if a group falls short of the minimum history requirement. We record these exclusions rather than quietly filling them with predictions, so the benchmark table reflects valid outputs under each model's own protocol, and the subgroup analysis shows exactly where coverage or error gets uneven.

We also keep model selection strictly separate from evidence generation. Validation data drive hyperparameter choices and conformal adjustments; the 2025 holdout is touched only for final metrics, interval coverage, subgroup summaries, and diagnostics --- never to pick between quantile levels, retune the production estimators, or revise the feature contract. That ordering matters most for uncertainty reporting: an interval can look well calibrated on a reused sample and still fail to hold on a genuinely untouched period. The frozen test is what keeps both point accuracy and risk-aware output honest.

\subsection{Uncertainty Calibration and Quantile Validity}
The uncertainty layer targets an 80\% prediction interval formed by the lower and upper quantiles. Before calibration, empirical test coverage is 78.24\% with mean prediction interval width 165.05 INR per quintal. Mondrian CQR increases coverage to 79.85\% and produces a mean width of 166.91 INR per quintal. The calibrated result lands close to the nominal target, though that's no guarantee it'll hold under future distribution shift.

Independent quantile regressors can violate the required ordering. On the test set, 2,942 of 19,303 raw prediction triplets contain a crossing, corresponding to 15.24\%. Monotonic rearrangement reduces the post-processing count to zero. This is a structural fix, not a calibration step --- rearrangement just enforces order, while CQR separately uses held-out residuals to adjust interval width.

\subsection{Ablation and Market-Group Behavior}
The ablation evidence shows that price history is the dominant information source, but exogenous variables provide complementary signal. Removing price-lag and momentum features increases MAE from the full-model reference to INR 100.37 per quintal and raises MAPE to 1.53\%. Removing arrival features yields MAE 78.71 and MAPE 1.07\%, while removing festival features yields MAE 66.10 and MAPE 0.90\%. The weather-and-arrivals results need care, since removing them shifts several correlated variables at once --- they point to complementary predictive value, not causation.

The commodity breakdown is heterogeneous. LightGBM P50 obtains its smallest errors on Maize, Paddy, Potato, and Wheat, while Chilli Red and Mustard show substantially larger errors. That lines up with what we already know --- high-volatility spice and cash-crop series tend to inflate the pooled error. The model is therefore evaluated at both aggregate and subgroup levels rather than relying on the overall score alone.

\subsection{Statistical Tests and Residual Diagnostics}
The Diebold--Mariano suite uses Newey--West heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation-consistent covariance. Under absolute-error loss, LightGBM and Ridge differ significantly, with the sign and frozen interpretation indicating Ridge as the superior point forecaster. Under the same loss, LightGBM and XGBoost also differ significantly, while the comparison with CatBoost depends on the loss used. These tests aren't meant to crown a universal winner --- they simply quantify how forecasts differ under a given loss.

Ljung--Box diagnostics indicate residual autocorrelation at lags 7, 14, and 30 with $p<0.001$. This pattern is consistent with periodic auction cycles and unobserved calendar or policy effects remaining in the residuals. That leftover autocorrelation is a reminder that a high $R^2$ doesn't mean the forecasting problem is fully solved. It also motivates future work on explicit hierarchical and seasonal residual models.

\subsection{Operational Anomaly Triage}
Isolation Forest is trained as an unsupervised operational filter on arrival-ratio, arrival-velocity, price-volatility, and price-spread signals. In the frozen test scope it flags 1,286 observations, or 6.66\% of the test rows. The repository evaluates these flags against proxy rules based on extreme arrival ratios or negative price velocity rather than against verified historical event labels. The resulting specificity of 96.05\%, precision proxy of 45.10\%, recall proxy of 40.47\%, and F1 proxy of 42.66\% therefore describe agreement with an operational heuristic, not supervised classification accuracy.

This matters for deployment: a flagged observation is a signal for an analyst to review, not proof a supply shock actually happened. In the interface, the anomaly channel should be displayed alongside the price interval and its provenance, allowing users to distinguish a forecast, an uncertainty estimate, and an operational warning.
\subsection{Fairness and Spatial Transfer}
By ``fairness'' here, we mean comparable evaluation conditions --- not identical hyperparameters across models. Each algorithm uses a model-appropriate objective and architecture, but all models must predict the same next-day target under a documented chronological design. The final analysis should report whether any commodity or mandi receives materially worse errors, whether sequence models lose observations because of their windows, and whether ARIMA excludes groups because of insufficient history.

\subsection{Interpretability}
TreeSHAP is used for feature attribution in tree models \cite{lundberg2020local}. Feature attribution describes patterns, not causes. Grouped Granger tests can show predictive precedence, but they don't establish an actual physical mechanism \cite{granger1969investigating}. The paper should distinguish these interpretations when discussing weather, arrivals, NDVI, or festival variables.

\section{Operational Integration}
The production registry loads an active version and requires P10, P50, and P90 model artifacts together with metadata. It rejects anything incomplete or inconsistent --- a partial quantile bundle, missing feature metadata, the wrong feature order, an incorrect target declaration, or a mismatched feature count. Startup validates that the serving dataset is non-empty, has valid dates, and contains the required model columns.

Live sync no longer invents values for weather, NDVI, price bounds, variety, or grade when the real data is missing. Incomplete live rows are skipped and logged. Cache warming requires both models and the serving dataset to be initialized. Alert dispatch similarly skips incomplete forecasts instead of sending invented prices or decisions.

These controls matter because an offline metric alone doesn't prove production readiness. The final certification must verify both the model outputs and the ability of the API to load the same artifacts without entering degraded mode.

\subsection{Reproducibility and Artifact Certification}
The repository treats reproducibility as an artifact property rather than only a narrative claim. The experiment manifest records the dataset path, date range, split sizes, commodity list, mandi list, feature count, target variable, forecast horizon, random seed, and evaluation rules. The claims matrix then maps each proposed scientific statement to a concrete experiment, result summary, confidence level, and allowed wording. That separation matters because a number can be technically correct and still get overinterpreted if its evaluation scope isn't made clear.

The final model bundle contains the three quantile estimators and metadata describing the active feature contract. The registry checks that the bundle is complete and that the model version, target declaration, feature count, and feature ordering agree. It does not silently load a partial bundle as a production model. The same principle applies to serving data: the API verifies that required model columns are present and that dates are valid before generating predictions.

These controls also define the boundary of the paper’s claims. A successful local compilation, model file, or API health response does not by itself establish predictive validity. Predictive claims lean on the frozen test metrics, deployment claims on successful artifact loading and serving checks, and anomaly claims on being upfront that the labels are heuristic, not ground truth. The paper consequently reports methodology, predictive evidence, and operational readiness as related but distinct forms of evidence.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
The results come with several limitations worth stating plainly. The strongest aggregate point forecast is Ridge, not the quantile LightGBM system --- the production model earns its place through uncertainty estimation and operational output, not through the lowest MAE. Relatedly, modal prices are highly persistent, which makes naive and linear baselines unusually hard to beat; performance here should be read relative to those baselines rather than against some arbitrary zero-error ideal.

The residual tests also show significant periodic autocorrelation at lags 7, 14, and 30, likely from unobserved auction schedules, macroeconomic announcements, procurement policy shifts, and market closures that never made it into the error process. On top of that, Chilli Red, Mustard, and the Azadpur market all show notably larger errors than the rest --- enough to rule out any claim that the system transfers uniformly across every agricultural market.

LSTM, GRU, and TFT remain proof-of-concept sequence baselines, never tuned with the same budget as the production quantile models. These runs simply weren't pushed to their full potential --- that says nothing about whether deep learning could work well here with proper tuning. Isolation Forest, similarly, is an unsupervised triage component evaluated against proxy heuristics rather than verified historical shock labels, so it shouldn't be read as a supervised classifier. And the COVID-period analysis is retrospective and in-sample, since the 2020--2021 observations were part of the training pool --- it's not a genuine test of generalizing to an unseen future crisis.

Finally, conformal calibration is estimated from historical validation data and can degrade under distribution shift. The 47-feature contract limits feature-order drift but does not remove measurement error, missingness, multicollinearity, or upstream-provider changes. Future work should evaluate online recalibration, external-market validation, hierarchical commodity--mandi models, stronger probabilistic sequence baselines, and event-labeled anomaly evaluation while preserving strict test isolation.

\section{Conclusion}
CropLens AI presents a repository-grounded framework for next-day agricultural modal-price forecasting across ten commodities and ten Indian mandis. Its core methodological contribution is bringing all of this together: a strict next-day target, an explicit 47-feature contract, chronological test isolation, independent multi-quantile LightGBM models, monotonic rearrangement, validation-only conformal calibration, and a broad benchmark and diagnostic suite.

The frozen 2025 evaluation yields an important qualified conclusion. Ridge provides the lowest aggregate point error, while LightGBM supplies the calibrated lower, median, and upper forecasts needed for uncertainty-aware decisions. The CQR interval reaches 79.85\% empirical coverage against an 80\% target, and rearrangement eliminates all observed post-processing quantile crossings. Subgroup and spatial results show meaningful but nonuniform transfer, and residual autocorrelation demonstrates that the market process retains unexplained temporal structure. These findings back up a reproducible decision-support system --- but they stop short of proving universal model superiority, causal weather effects, supervised-level anomaly detection, or generalization to crises the model has never seen.
